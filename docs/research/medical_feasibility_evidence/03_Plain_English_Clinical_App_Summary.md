### 1. Introduction
Simple AAC is a mobile software application developed for Android devices to support communication for individuals who experience severe difficulty producing intelligible spoken language. The application has been designed specifically within the socio-cultural and linguistic context of Sri Lanka. It provides a digital communication board on which users can select visual pictures to communicate their immediate desires, needs, and emotional states. The application is completely free of charge, requires no recurring subscriptions, and operates locally on the mobile device without needing an ongoing internet connection.

### 2. Purpose of the Application
The primary purpose of Simple AAC is to provide an accessible, low-cost augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) option for families and healthcare centres in Sri Lanka. In low- and middle-income settings, commercial dedicated electronic communication aids and proprietary AAC software packages are often prohibitively expensive or unavailable in local languages. Simple AAC was created to bridge this gap by turning everyday Android smartphones and tablets into customized visual communication systems. The application aims to help non-verbal or minimally verbal children express their basic everyday needs, thereby reducing the communicative frustration that often leads to emotional distress or behavioural challenges.

### 3. Main Communication Features
The application interface is centred around a visual grid of communication cards. Each card displays a clear picture, a written word, and an associated background colour:
- **Card Selection and Spoken Feedback:** When a user taps a card, the application highlights the tile and produces spoken audio corresponding to the card label.
- **Hierarchical Folders:** Cards can be organized into thematic categories or nested inside folders. For example, selecting a "Food" card can open a sub-grid containing specific items such as rice, bread, or fruit.
- **Communication Sentence Strip:** Across the top of the screen, the application features a persistent sentence strip. When cards are tapped, they are added in sequence to this strip. Users can press a speaker button to play the entire sequence aloud, allowing them to construct simple multi-word requests, such as "I want" followed by "Water".
- **Vocabulary Customisation:** Caregivers and therapists can create new cards directly on the handset. The application allows adults to capture photographs of real household objects using the device camera, import pictures from the gallery, or select animated image clips.
- **Caregiver Controls:** The application includes a board-locking feature that prevents children from accidentally closing the app or changing settings. Access to editing tools and system configuration can be secured using the device's fingerprint scanner, screen lock, or a four-digit security PIN.

### 4. Relevance to Children with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)
Children with Autism Spectrum Disorder frequently present with qualitative impairments in verbal communication, social interaction, and sensory processing. Many autistic children process visual information more effectively than transient auditory speech. Simple AAC aligns with established visual communication practices, such as the Picture Exchange Communication System (PECS), by translating abstract spoken words into concrete, static visual symbols. 

Furthermore, children with ASD often exhibit significant sensory sensitivity or visual distractibility. Simple AAC provides adjustments to accommodate these needs:
- The visual density of the board can be adjusted from a simple single-column display (suitable for beginners) up to a five-column grid (for advanced communicators).
- Unneeded cards can be temporarily hidden without being deleted, allowing caregivers to introduce vocabulary gradually.
- Tactile vibration feedback confirms when a touch is registered, assisting children who have fine-motor difficulties or poor touch registration.
- High-contrast visual modes and colour-vision adjustments are available for children with co-occurring visual processing challenges.

Importantly, emotional expression is supported through a dedicated "Feelings" category, allowing children to communicate states such as "Happy", "Sad", or "Angry". However, the software does not contain automated facial recognition or emotional artificial intelligence; emotional communication remains entirely directed by the user.

### 5. Sinhala, Tamil, and English Support
Simple AAC is designed to support Sri Lanka's trilingual environment. Every communication card can store labels in English, Sinhala, and Tamil. The user can switch the active language through the settings drawer, which immediately updates the written text displayed on the screen. 

To support local families, the software incorporates an offline database of 179 common daily communication concepts pre-translated across all three languages. When an adult types an English word like "water", the system automatically populates the Sinhala (වතුර) and Tamil (தண்ணீர்) equivalents. 

Spoken output is handled through two methods:
1. **Device Synthetic Speech:** The app directs text to the Android text-to-speech engine. While English speech is universally supported, synthetic Sinhala and Tamil speech rely entirely on whether the family's Android device has local language speech packages installed by the manufacturer.
2. **Recorded Human Audio:** To overcome limitations in synthetic speech, caregivers can record their own voices directly into any card using the device microphone. When tapped, the card plays the recorded voice note. This ensures that authentic local pronunciations, familiar dialects, and comforting parental voices can always be heard.

### 6. Practical Use by Children and Caregivers
In practice, the application is intended to be used collaboratively. A parent, teacher, or therapist introduces the device during natural daily routines, such as mealtimes or play sessions. When a child wishes to make a request, they tap the corresponding card, which immediately voices the request aloud. 

Because children with autism may explore devices by tapping rapidly or attempting to switch apps, the "Pin" feature locks the screen so that the communication board remains visible. The caregiver lock ensures that editing menus cannot be altered by the child. The application stores all data locally, meaning that families can use the tool at home, in the classroom, or while travelling by bus without needing a mobile data connection.

### 7. Professional Involvement and Clinical Considerations
Although Simple AAC has been built using recognized principles of visual communication, it is a tool rather than a standalone clinical intervention. The application cannot replace individualized assessment and therapy provided by speech and language therapists, paediatricians, or psychiatrists. Successful adoption requires professional guidance to determine whether a child has the visual discrimination, pointing ability, and cognitive readiness required for direct-selection tablet AAC. Professionals are also needed to guide families on how to structure the vocabulary to avoid overwhelming the child.

### 8. Technical Feasibility
The software has reached an advanced stage of technical stability on the Android operating system (Android 7.0 through Android 16). In laboratory testing and automated checks across 210 software tests, the application reliably preserves user data, saves voice recordings, and operates without crashing. Battery consumption is minimal, and application backups can be exported as standard archive files to the device storage. Conversely, the application has not been adapted or verified for Apple iOS devices and must be evaluated exclusively on Android hardware.

### 9. Areas Requiring Further Assessment
Before Simple AAC can be recommended for clinical or educational use, several vital questions must be evaluated through formal research:
1. **Clinical Suitability:** We must investigate whether children with ASD in Sri Lanka can independently use the touchscreen interface to communicate their needs in clinical and home environments.
2. **Caregiver Acceptability:** We must examine whether parents find the card creation and category organization manageable, and whether using the app causes any caregiver stress.
3. **Linguistic and Cultural Appropriateness:** The 179 pre-translated vocabulary items must be formally reviewed by speech therapists and linguists to ensure they reflect natural everyday usage across different Sri Lankan communities.
4. **Hardware and Voice Availability:** We must determine how frequently local Android devices lack offline Sinhala and Tamil synthetic speech engines, and whether families find voice recording practical.
5. **Therapeutic Outcomes:** Rigorous prospective studies are required to determine whether using the app actually improves functional communication, reduces behavioural frustration, or supports language development over time.
